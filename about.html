01 / The map

Trust the process. First, have one.

Creative thinking becomes easier to practise when we can see the moves behind it. The Elements of Surprise makes those moves visible, giving people across disciplines more ways to approach a problem, develop an idea and get beyond their first answer.

a part of Something Clever, it brings 60 creative moves into one practical atlas, a place to start when you need a different way forward.

02 / How to read it

A move, applied at a layer.

Columns describe the operation: combine, map, add, remove, swap, divide, reorder, scale, invert or loop.

Rows describe where you apply it:

Surface
The material, appearance or sensory qualities.
Context
Where, when and how people encounter it.
Structure
How its parts fit and work together.
Frame
The perspective or meaning attached to it.
Rules
The constraints and conventions it operates within.
Purpose
The job it does and what it is trying to achieve.
One operation. Two different moves.

Remove + Surface = Strip. Remove detail so one element carries the message.

Remove + Rules = Break a rule. Remove a convention to make room for a different approach.

The columns sit in three navigation groups: Connect brings ideas together or carries them between fields; Edit changes the parts; Reshape changes order, scale, direction or repetition.

Hover over a cell for a short description, or select it to read the full explanation and five real examples. On mobile, tap a cell to open it.

03 / Find a starting point

Start with what you need.

Browse freely, or open the filter button at the top right. Choose the situation closest to yours:

Work on a brief
Choose what needs to happen: get noticed, make something clear, make it matter, build belief, encourage action or create more value.
Get unstuck
Choose the obstacle, whether the idea feels generic, too complicated, irrelevant or difficult to demonstrate.
Explore
Get six varied moves from across the map. “Another six” gives you a different set.

You can add one optional refinement, then apply your focus. Six suggested moves stay highlighted; the rest remain available in grey. Open a highlighted move to see a short “Why this move?” explanation.

These are starting points chosen to suit the need you selected. Follow one, combine several or clear your focus to explore the whole table.

04 / Use a move

Make it specific to your problem.

  1. Name the job. Write down what you need the idea to achieve, for whom and in what situation.
  2. Choose a move. Read its description and examples until the underlying operation is clear.
  3. Apply it to your subject. Try a few versions. For Strip: “What could we remove so the important part becomes easier to see?”
  4. Check the result. Does it help with the original job? Keep, combine or revise what works.

The examples show how a move works. Borrow the principle and apply it to the material in front of you.

05 / The thinking behind it

Making creativity easier to work with.

The project takes inspiration from TRIZ’s systematic approach to inventive problem-solving and Margaret Boden’s account of creativity.

Boden describes three forms: combinational makes new connections between familiar ideas; exploratory finds possibilities within existing rules; transformational changes the rules to make new possibilities available.

The table’s groups organise the moves. Boden’s framework helps describe how you use them. The same move can explore an existing approach or help invent a new one.

This is a working atlas. Moves can overlap, and one idea may use several. Its purpose is to make more approaches visible and easier to try.
